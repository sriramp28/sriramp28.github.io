About

Aerospace Foundations, Earth-System Questions.

I am a computational geophysical fluid dynamicist and educator studying how interfaces, stratification, and turbulence govern the movement of heat and matter through the environment. My current work combines high-resolution ocean simulation with hydrographic observations to examine double-diffusive mixing, polar heat pathways, finite-depth transport, and what sparse measurements can reveal about evolving fluid structure.

I build numerical models, run large simulations, develop analysis algorithms, and connect dense computed fields to physical arguments and observable quantities. The common question across this work is how multiscale transport can be represented faithfully enough to explain a natural system, test a model, or guide an observing strategy.

My training in aerospace and mechanical engineering provides the methodological foundation for that program. Earlier research on propulsion, reacting shear layers, acoustics, thermal loading, and coupled CFD/FEA taught me to work across governing equations, numerical scales, and practical constraints. Those same habits now support questions in ocean, polar, atmospheric, riverine, and other environmental flows.

I also teach aerodynamics, computational fluid dynamics, fluid mechanics, and computing with the same emphasis on physical interpretation. Whether the topic is lift generation, finite-difference stability, odd-even decoupling in the heat equation, or simulation workflow, I try to help students see both the mathematics and the physical consequence of a model.

I am especially interested in research and teaching opportunities in geophysical fluid dynamics, Earth-system science, ocean and atmospheric dynamics, environmental transport, and computational science, including work that connects first-principles models with field observations.

Research direction

Transport across environmental interfaces.

My work combines governing-equation models, high-performance computation, environmental observations, and scale-aware analysis. I study how fluid interfaces select transport pathways, how boundary geometry and background flow alter those pathways, and which signatures survive realistic sampling. This creates a direct bridge between process-resolving simulation and the measurements used to understand the ocean and the broader Earth system.

Scientific range

Natural and engineered fluid systems.

The physical thread through my work is transport across structured, unstable, or reacting interfaces. It connects salt-finger layers and polar thermohaline structure to turbulent shear layers, combustors, aeroacoustics, and thermal-fluid design. I am building from this foundation toward a broader program in ocean, atmospheric, riverine, and lacustrine dynamics, where process-resolving computation and field data can be used together to understand environmental change.

Communication

From analysis to usable physical judgment.

I care about turning complex simulations into evidence that can guide scientific and technical judgment. That means nondimensionalization, careful visualization, quantitative comparison, reproducible workflows, and writing that connects diagnostics to physical interpretation, design tradeoffs, mechanical constraints, thermal margins, and model limitations. The same communication style carries into teaching, where numerical artifacts, governing equations, and computed fields have to become ideas students can reason with independently.

Service and life outside work

Campus service and creative practice.

At Georgia Tech, I serve on several advisory committees in different capacities, with work spanning student experience, graduate education, academic policy, and community-facing service. Outside the office, I am an avid archer, runner, musician, linguist, and poet.
