About

Fluid Dynamics Across Natural and Engineered Systems.

I am a computational fluid dynamicist and educator studying how interfaces, coherent structures, and instabilities organize the movement of heat, momentum, chemical species, and salt. My research spans high-speed reacting flows, propulsion, acoustics, stratified oceans, double-diffusive mixing, and sparse observation.

These application areas are different in scale and purpose, but neither is secondary to the other. A reacting shear layer and a thermohaline interface both ask how localized structures control exchange, which mechanisms survive reduced resolution, and what a practical measurement can recover from a complex three-dimensional flow.

I build numerical models, run large simulations, develop analysis algorithms, and connect dense computed fields to physical arguments and observable quantities. Direct and large-eddy simulation, conservation budgets, coherent-structure analysis, reduced models, uncertainty, and sparse sensing provide a common computational language across aerospace and geophysical fluid dynamics.

I completed the requirements for the Ph.D. in Aerospace Engineering at Georgia Tech and successfully defended my dissertation on August 14, 2026. My training also includes graduate degrees in mathematics, mechanical engineering, and aerospace engineering, along with research experience in high-performance computation, combustion, thermal-fluid systems, and environmental transport.

I also teach aerodynamics, computational fluid dynamics, fluid mechanics, and computing with the same emphasis on physical interpretation. Whether the topic is lift generation, finite-difference stability, odd-even decoupling in the heat equation, or simulation workflow, I try to help students see both the mathematics and the physical consequence of a model.

I am interested in research and teaching environments where careful computation can clarify difficult fluid problems, whether the setting is an aircraft engine, a high-speed shear layer, an ocean interface, or an environmental transport system.

Research direction

Two domains, one physical thread.

My work combines governing-equation models, high-performance computation, mathematical diagnostics, and observations. I study how flow structures select transport pathways, how geometry and operating conditions alter those pathways, and which signatures survive reduced models or realistic sampling. The same framework supports design and diagnosis in aerospace systems as well as process understanding in oceans and the environment.

Scientific range

Natural and engineered fluid systems.

The physical thread through my work is transport across structured, unstable, stratified, or reacting interfaces. It connects turbulent shear layers, combustors, aeroacoustics, and thermal-fluid design with salt-finger layers, polar thermohaline structure, and environmental exchange. Each domain offers distinct questions and an opportunity to test which computational ideas remain broadly useful.

Communication

From analysis to usable physical judgment.

I care about turning complex simulations into evidence that can guide scientific and technical judgment. That means nondimensionalization, careful visualization, quantitative comparison, reproducible workflows, and writing that connects diagnostics to physical interpretation, design tradeoffs, mechanical constraints, thermal margins, and model limitations. The same communication style carries into teaching, where numerical artifacts, governing equations, and computed fields have to become ideas students can reason with independently.

Service and life outside work

Campus service and creative practice.

At Georgia Tech, I serve on several advisory committees in different capacities, with work spanning student experience, graduate education, academic policy, and community-facing service. Outside the office, I am an avid archer, runner, musician, linguist, and poet.
